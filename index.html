The useful basics

Keep it
simple.
Keep it safe.

Strength training can be part of a healthy routine for young people when it’s taught and supervised well. Start with skills, then build gradually.[1]

01

Learn before you load

Ask a qualified coach, PE teacher, or experienced adult to show you how equipment works. Begin with a weight you can move with control. Stop if something hurts sharply or feels wrong.[1]

02

Practice the same patterns

A beginner session can use a few basic movement patterns: push, pull, squat, hinge, and carry. You don’t need a complicated split to learn how your body moves.

03

Progress at a patient pace

When your technique feels steady and the work feels manageable, add a small challenge: a little weight, a repetition, or better control. Avoid testing your one-rep max as a beginner.[1]

04

Make room for all movement

Gym training is one option, not a requirement. WHO guidance for ages 5–17 includes an average of 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, with muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days. Walking, sport, dance, and play count too.[2]

    Between sessions

    Rest is part
    of the plan.

    Training is just one piece of wellbeing. Recovery habits should support your whole life: school, friends, mood, and movement.

    8–10 h

    Make sleep a priority

    Teens aged 13–18 are recommended to get 8–10 hours of sleep per 24 hours. A steady sleep routine can help make that easier.[3]

    Rest days count

    Leave space to recover

    You don’t have to train hard every day. Alternate harder sessions with easier movement or rest, and pay attention to how you feel.

    Everyday basics

    Eat enough. Drink to thirst.

    Regular meals and water support daily life and activity. Avoid extreme diets and supplement pressure; ask a parent/guardian or health professional about nutrition questions.

    Filter the noise

    Gym myths,
    meet reality.

    Fitness posts often turn nuanced science into one-line rules. Here are a few calmer corrections.

    MYTH 01“Lifting weights always stops teens growing.”

    What evidence says: Properly designed and supervised resistance training can be appropriate for young people. Safe instruction and technique matter.[1]

    MYTH 02“You need to train every day to make progress.”

    Try this instead: Consistency over time matters more than daily gym sessions. Activity can come from many places, and recovery belongs in a routine.[2]

    MYTH 03“Soreness means it was a good workout.”

    Reality: Soreness isn’t a scorecard. A useful session can feel manageable; sharp pain is a reason to stop and get help.

    MYTH 04“There’s one perfect beginner program.”

    Reality: A good starting point fits your experience, access, interests, and health. A qualified coach can help adapt movements to you.
